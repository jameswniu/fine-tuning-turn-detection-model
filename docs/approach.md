# End-of-Turn Detection, an approach

This document presents the solution and the reasoning. The repo carries the depth: POLICY.md holds the labeled turn policy, EVALS.md the gates and bands, data/README.md the dataset provenance, and iterations.md the audit trail of every training run.

## The problem, priced

A turn detector can be wrong in exactly two ways. Speak too early and it interrupts the caller, the error people remember. Wait too long and it leaves dead air, the error every turn pays a little of. A trivial system is perfect on one axis, so a single accuracy number means nothing here. The real decision is where to sit on the tradeoff curve. I made it explicit before training anything: one interruption costs as much caller annoyance as five sluggish responses. The ratio comes from my own production voice agent on a real phone number, where dead air, not interruptions, was the chronic breach. It is written down so you can disagree with it.

Run through the Bayes rule, a 1:5 ratio says speak at 83 percent confidence. Measured, the probabilities were not calibrated. The model runs under-confident, so the theoretical bar over-waits. The operating threshold therefore comes from the measured validation curve at the target cost ratio. It is recomputed every training run and ships as data next to the weights; the threshold is a dial, not a constant. Synthetic validation also failed at setting it, in both directions. The English lane's threshold drifted high against human judgment. The multilingual lane's leaked validation split reached zero cost and picked a degenerate 0.33. Final selection therefore moves to a small dev set built outside the training distribution, separate from the frozen test referee. How it is labeled is in the evaluation section.

## Data, policy first

I spent the most thought here, in this order: policy first, volume second.

First, a taxonomy of eleven pause situations. Seven anchors have obvious answers: complete statements, complete questions, a caller halfway through reading out a number. Four judgment classes have none: a complete sentence that might continue, trailing hedges, self-interrupted restarts, and a caller asking for time. I built a small labeling booth and blind-labeled sixty calibration cards, shuffled so the taxonomy could not bias me. Those labels are frozen as a gold set the model never trains on. That is the referee.

The judgment classes produced real policy. The one I would defend on a whiteboard is the hedge ruling. "The broker said it was covered, supposedly" and "the detention was approved, or something" share a shape and have opposite correct answers. The difference is ownership. A claim attributed to someone else is one the speaker does not own; the hedge is genuine doubt, so the agent speaks to confirm. A first-person claim with a trailing softener is settled; the hedge is politeness, and statement rules apply. Attribution markers against first-person assertion are surface features a text model can learn. That is what makes the ruling trainable rather than philosophy.

Then volume. A seeded, pure-code generator expands the policy into training data with three robustness moves. Complete utterances are re-emitted truncated mid-sentence and labeled wait, because that is what ASR partials look like. Every sample also ships lowercased with terminal punctuation stripped, so the model cannot cheat off periods. Every contexted sample is also emitted bare, so the utterance carries its own signal. No LLM sits in the generation loop; the dataset regenerates byte-identically, and every line can be audited. The scale-up generator reuses the same template banks verbatim, since the templates are the policy encoding and must not fork. Its volume comes from larger slot pools: generated spoken numbers, more cities, more times.

The multilingual bonus is Spanish, chosen for the domain. A large share of US carriers run Spanish-first. The banks use the register those calls actually have: Mexican-Spanish trucking speech with real Spanglish, "el rate con", "la troca", "el dispatch". The hedge ruling transfers because Spanish has the same attribution markers, "me dijeron que", "según ellos". The ASR variant also strips inverted punctuation, which matters more than in English: it removes the question cue from the front of the utterance.

Why created instead of found, in one breath. Public conversational corpora carry the wrong distribution, chitchat and meetings rather than dispatch calls, plus no per-class policy labels and licensing friction. The part that needed human judgment, the sixty gold cards and the class policies, was small enough to do properly by hand. Provenance for all four data files lives in data/README.md.

## Models, three lanes

The build trains three models through one recipe and lets the measured curve pick what ships.

The first lane fine-tunes DistilBERT, 66M parameters, English. It classifies the last agent line plus the caller's words so far. It is the safe recipe the open-source state of the art validates.

The second lane taught me the most. It trains from random initialization with its own tokenizer, trained on the task corpus itself, bilingual, accents kept, feeding a small BERT-shaped encoder of 3.7M parameters. The classification recipe tracks the fine-tune lane, same loss, schedule, selection, and export, so the comparison isolates pretraining. The hypothesis was reasonable: turn-finality lives mostly in surface syntax, connectors, disfluencies, and attribution markers, a narrow enough family that a small model might not need pretraining.

It failed twice, and each failure bought a finding. The first attempt used WordPiece, which learned only 1532 pieces because a template corpus has few unique words. Text the templates never produced tokenized into walls of unknown tokens. The model emitted a constant on it: 0.9998 on synthetic validation, a coin-flip 0.50 on the human gold set. The second attempt fixed both mechanical causes. Byte-level BPE, so nothing can be out of vocabulary. And a template-grouped validation split, after catching that a row-level split puts fills of the same template on both sides and measures memorization as generalization. With the mechanics honest, the verdict got harder: 0.99 on a held-out slice of the same synthetic distribution, still 0.50 on gold. Coverage was no longer the problem. Knowledge was.

Two ingredients then moved it, and how far each moved it is the finding. A few hundred real caller turns took gold from 0.50 to 0.994, above every fine-tuned lane. What did not move exposes the mechanism: unseen real calls stayed near 0.60. The real rows had broken the model's collapse on unfamiliar text, not taught it language. Language came from the second ingredient, a fifteen-minute masked-language-model pretrain of our own, on Wikipedia slices in both languages plus the task corpus, tokenizer included. Warm-started from that base, the model reads 0.973 on gold, a perfect Spanish slice, and 0.825 on unseen real calls. It also holds the widest guardrail-safe window in the build: sixty admissible thresholds, against fifty-eight for the incumbent and four for the multilingual lane. The full curve on unseen real calls, one named ingredient per step: 0.48 random init, 0.60 with real data, 0.825 with our own pretrain, 0.913 where the internet-pretrained fine-tune sits. The last gap is what a few billion pretraining tokens buy over our twenty-five megabytes. One tradeoff stays on the record: pretraining cost a sliver of gold, 0.994 down to 0.973 and one interruption card, to buy the 23 points on real calls. Both versions stay on the scoreboard because the tradeoff is the story.

<p align="center"><img src="../assets/pretrain-curve.svg" alt="What pretraining is worth, measured as PR-AUC on unseen real calls: 0.48 random init, 0.60 adding real calls, 0.83 adding a fifteen-minute pretrain, 0.91 web-pretrained" width="100%"></p>

The shipped default is picked by a written rule, not taste. Scratch ships only if it lands within about 0.02 gold PR-AUC of the fine-tune, keeps false-speak at zero, holds recall within a few points, and does not collapse on the out-of-distribution slice below. The rule decided against it, narrowly, for one reason. The pretrained scratch model leads or ties on gold, Spanish, guardrail headroom, calibration, and latency. But its 0.825 on unseen real calls is not parity with the fine-tune's 0.913, and the real-call referee is the one production trusts. So the fine-tuned English model ships as the default, 0.949 on gold with zero false-speak and 0.913 on real calls. The scratch model stands as the fastest, cleanest lane, and its remaining gap is the thing production data volume closes. The full scoreboard sits at the end of the evaluation section.

The third lane fine-tunes multilingual DistilBERT on the bilingual corpus, and the numbers justify it. On English gold it matches the English-only model, 0.960 against 0.958 PR-AUC, so the multilingual vocabulary costs nothing where it matters. On the held-out Spanish slice the English-only model manages 0.911 with recall barely above half; the multilingual fine-tune separates it perfectly. Spanish support is a model swap, not a rebuild. Policy, generators, evals, and serving carry over unchanged.

## Evaluation

<p align="center"><img src="../assets/referees.svg" alt="Three referees, one question each: a frozen gold set of 60 human-labeled cards for generalization, 6 probe-found regressions for memory, and 96 held-out real-call turns for discovery" width="100%"></p>

The referee structure matters more than any single number. The frozen gold set grades quality and is never trained on. A separate dev set picks thresholds, so the test referee stays untouched by selection. The dev set is labeled by a certified judge panel: three independent models blind-label a shuffled batch, each certified by agreement with my sixty frozen gold labels before its dev votes count, majority rules, disagreements marked unsure, provenance recorded. One caveat travels with that: the policy doc quotes a handful of gold rulings, so the certification exam was partially open-book and is read accordingly.

<p align="center"><img src="../assets/judges.svg" alt="How the dev set was labeled and why to trust it: 60 gold cards with known human answers hidden among 30 fresh cards, three stock vendor judges with zero training, two-of-three majority, and the output feeds one file that tunes one number clamped by 12 human gates" width="100%"></p>

A regression slice holds every failure found by live probing, a hard gate that a fix stays fixed. A held-out Spanish slice at a different generator seed grades the bilingual claim.

Then the referee that changed the build. Everything above lives near one distribution. So I pulled sixty real production calls from my own voice agent and let them label themselves: the point where the caller actually stopped is a true complete, and a mid-turn prefix of the same utterance is a true wait. The discriminator is exact, calls initiated through Twilio and longer than the watchdog pings. Four hundred rows, split by call: two thirds available for training augmentation, one third locked as the real-call referee. The labels carry stated noise. The vendor's own turn-taker drew the boundaries, and a random cut can land where a phrase happens to sound complete, so the numbers read as pessimistic bounds. Excluding sentence-boundary cuts moved the headline by only two points, which is how I know the noise is not the story. The story: the fine-tuned model, 0.96 and zero false-speak on gold, scored 0.61 on real calls with false-speak around forty percent. Real callers hesitate, restart, and trail off in ways neither my templates nor my hand-written gold cards do. Every offline referee agreed with each other, and the real world disagreed with all of them.

The fix ran inside the build. The training-side two thirds went back into the data mix as real-register augmentation, graded on the locked third. It worked on every lane it touched. The shipping fine-tune went from 0.65 to 0.913 on the real referee, with recall 0.959. The multilingual lane landed at 0.875. The scratch lane's movement is the pretraining curve above. One correction to the referee itself is named. Turns where the vendor's turn-taker answered against our written policy would import its behavior into the labels, so the slice builder relabels those to wait, marked and counted. This pull matched zero verbatim phrases. The one real poisoning incident traced to softer register patterns instead, caught by a tier-1 gate going red and fixed with data, not string filters.

The raw call content never enters the repo. The slice files stay local; only aggregates appear here.

Two baselines set the floor. Always-speak-on-pause, which is what a plain VAD timeout does, interrupts on every true wait in the gold set. A punctuation heuristic is respectable on clean text and collapses on ASR-style text. The gates and bands the loop iterates against live in EVALS.md, with industry anchors verified against LiveKit's and Pipecat's published numbers.

The verdict follows the written rule. The fine-tuned English model ships as the default because it leads the referee production trusts. The pretrained scratch model is the guardrail-cleanest and fastest lane, and the measured argument for what pretraining is worth.

## Serving and latency

FastAPI over ONNX Runtime, dynamically quantized to int8, CPU only, one worker by default. The endpoint returns the probability, the decision at the operating threshold, the threshold in force, and the model latency, on every request. The same process serves a live probe page that re-scores as you type; several training-data gaps were found there. The operating threshold is read from the model directory, so a retrain updates the dial without touching serving code. A Dockerfile builds the serving image with only the int8 model inside.

Measured on my machine, an M5 Pro laptop, single uvicorn worker, int8 on CPU, 1200 requests per level. The machine was in its late-night post-training thermal state, so these read conservative.

| Served artifact | c1 wall p50 | c8 wall p50 | c8 wall p95 | c8 req/s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fine-tuned DistilBERT, 66M | 34.7 ms | 45.5 ms | 57.9 ms | 170 |
| Multilingual DistilBERT, 134M | 34.0 ms | 49.7 ms | 97.2 ms | 147 |
| From-scratch, 7.4M | 7.4 ms | 12.1 ms | 20.7 ms | 588 |

The stress harness is an async client hammering the real endpoint at stepped concurrency. At concurrency 32 a single worker saturates; scaling past it is a worker-count knob, not a model problem. The brief asked for under 100 milliseconds per request. The shipping model clears it at 57.9 ms p95 under eight-way concurrency, and the from-scratch lane clears it five times over. Seven times faster at a fifth of the hardware per call is the standing argument for finishing the from-scratch road as production volume closes its accuracy gap.

## Monitoring at a million calls a month

Monitoring here means tracing decisions and observing failures until they convert into evals and guardrails, not dashboards to stare at. Every decision emits a trace: turn id, conversation context, the prefix probability trajectory rather than just the final score, the decision, the threshold in force, model latency, and what the caller did next.

That last field is the special thing about this problem. Turn detection grades itself in production, seconds later, for free. A speak decision followed by the caller talking over the agent within about half a second was a false speak. A wait decision followed by dead air until a timeout forced the response was a false wait. Those two rates are the online eval with no annotation cost, and they map one to one onto the offline curve. A small weekly human-audited sample keeps the auto-labels honest and feeds retraining: the same labeling booth pointed at sampled production turns, biased toward low-confidence decisions and every barge-in. Slice everything by language, accent, connection quality, and customer, because turn-taking drifts differently per slice. Alarm on score-distribution drift.

Shipping stays boring. A candidate model first replays the recorded archive offline and shows its disagreements with the incumbent. Then it shadows live traffic, scoring silently next to the incumbent while disagreement cases get mined for labels. Promotion happens on the measured curve. Rollback is a pointer flip to the previous checkpoint.

And the brief's direct question, whether we are limited by the datasets previously built. Yes, deliberately, and only until launch. My synthesis encodes policy; production volume supplies distribution. At almost a million calls a month, the handoff from one to the other is the whole monitoring design. The limit dissolves the day traces start converting into cases.

## The discussion questions, answered in advance

The brief's five questions are answered here. The rest of what a reviewer is likely to raise, including why DistilBERT, why int8 and what it cost, why 0.42, where verification actually happens, and an explicit list of what was not measured, sits in collapsed blocks at the bottom of the README.

On the limits of the current solution. The ceiling of a text-only detector is prosody, and it sits in the input rather than the model. Some utterances read identically whether the caller is finished or inhaling for more. That signal lives in pitch decline, final lengthening, and breath, and none of it reaches a text model. The system also inherits the transcriber's cadence, since a decision can only update when a partial arrives. On data: synthetic generation covers the policy but compresses the diversity of real callers. The gold referee is sixty cards, so per-class readings carry wide intervals. And the policy encodes one operator's judgment, mine, a feature for coherence and a limit for customers whose callers behave differently.

On audio against text against multimodal, and why any of them beat VAD. A VAD knows only that sound stopped, so it buys safety with silence, and every setting of that timeout is wrong for half the cases. Text sees what was said: syntax, connectors, attribution. That is most of the turn signal, and it is cheap to train, fast to serve, and easy to debug, which is why I started there. Audio sees how it was said, catches the prosody cases text cannot, and can act before transcription lands. Multimodal is the real frontier and my next step, a small audio encoder fused with the text signal at the decision layer. The eval harness built here, the frozen referee, the cost ratio, the production proxies, transfers to it unchanged. That is the point of building the referee first.

The reference architecture makes the limit visible as wiring. One arrow enters the turn detector and it carries final transcription, while raw audio flows past to the interruption handler. So the ceiling belongs to the input, not to the model, and no better text model recovers a falling pitch. The fix is a second arrow.

On whether a transcriber could do this job. Start with why it does not already. An ASR is trained to be invariant to prosody, because "yeah" spoken flat and "yeah" spoken rising have to produce the same token for the vocabulary to hold. That invariance is the product rather than a gap. The pitch information reaches the acoustic encoder and gets discarded at the text bottleneck, so the signal is available right up until the moment the model commits to words.

One tier of it is free today and nobody uses it. Vendor responses already carry word-level timestamps. Final-word lengthening and pause duration are among the strongest endpoint cues in the phonetics literature, and a text-only pipeline throws both away. Feeding them to the current model adds no model and no latency.

Past that, two ways. With Whisper, add an end-of-turn token to the decoder vocabulary and fine-tune on turn-annotated audio. At inference, read that token's probability against continuation tokens from the same forward pass that produces the transcript. Endpointing becomes a byproduct of transcription, and prosody arrives through the audio encoder. With a streaming RNN-T family model like Parakeet, train an end-of-utterance token into the transducer vocabulary, or hang a small classifier head off the encoder states. Both recipes need audio with true turn boundaries, which is exactly what the production self-labeling loop produces. The tradeoff is coupling: one model then owns two jobs on one latency budget. I would keep the detector separate while iterating fast, and consider distilling it into the transcriber once the policy stabilizes.

On integration into a voice agent. The detector sits between streaming ASR and the LLM trigger. Partials accumulate while the caller speaks. When VAD flags a pause candidate, the detector scores the last agent line plus the transcript so far, and the probability maps to an endpoint delay. A high score commits after a short guard. A middling score waits longer. A low score holds toward a hard ceiling and re-scores on every new partial. Barge-in handling is untouched. The model adds single-digit milliseconds co-located next to the orchestrator, well inside any partial cadence. Two refinements pay quickly. Smoothing across the prefix trajectory keeps the decision from flapping between partials. And speculative generation, starting LLM inference the moment the score goes high and cancelling if the caller resumes, converts detector confidence directly into answer latency.

## Assumptions, written down

The 1:5 cost ratio is a documented operating choice, not a law. It is one number, and re-deriving the threshold for a different ratio is a one-line change. Inputs arrive ASR-style, lowercased, no terminal punctuation, with the last agent utterance available as context. The decision is binary, speak or wait. Response length, and whether an acknowledgement ends a call, are downstream policies and deliberately out of scope. Languages covered are English and Spanish. The latency budget is 100 milliseconds end to end on CPU, no GPU assumed anywhere.
