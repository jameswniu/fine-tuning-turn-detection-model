# End-of-Turn Detection, an approach

This is the short document for the take-home. It presents the solution and the reasoning; the repo carries the depth (POLICY.md for the labeled policy, EVALS.md for the gates and bands, data/README.md for dataset provenance, iterations.md for the audit trail of every training run).

## The problem, priced

A turn detector can be wrong in exactly two ways. It speaks too early, which interrupts the caller, the error people remember. Or it waits too long, which is dead air, the error every single turn pays a little of. Any trivial system is perfect on one axis, so a single accuracy number means nothing here. The real decision is where to sit on the tradeoff curve, and I made that decision explicit before training anything. One interruption costs as much caller annoyance as five sluggish responses. That ratio comes from my own production voice agent on a real phone number, where the dashboards showed dead air, not interruptions, was the chronic breach, and it is written down precisely so you can disagree with it.

Run through the Bayes decision rule, a 1:5 ratio says speak when the model is at least 83 percent sure the turn is over. The eval then surfaced my favorite finding of the build. The formula is only correct if the probabilities are calibrated, and measured, they were not; the model runs under-confident, so the theoretical bar over-waits. The operating threshold therefore comes from the measured validation curve at the target cost ratio, recomputed every training run and shipped next to the weights. The threshold is a dial, not a constant. Synthetic validation also proved untrustworthy for setting it, in both directions: the English lane's threshold drifted high relative to human judgment, and the multilingual lane's leaked validation split reached zero cost and picked a degenerate 0.33. Final threshold selection therefore moves to a small dev set built outside the training distribution, kept separate from the frozen test referee; how it is labeled is in the evaluation section.

## Data, policy first

I spent the most thought here, and deliberately in this order, policy first, volume second.

First a taxonomy of eleven pause situations. Seven anchors where the answer is obvious, complete statements, complete questions, a caller halfway through reading out a number. Four judgment classes with no textbook answer, a complete sentence that might continue, trailing hedges, self-interrupted restarts, and a caller asking for time. I built a small labeling booth and blind-labeled sixty calibration cards myself, shuffled so the taxonomy could not bias me. Those labels are frozen as a gold set the model never trains on. That is the referee.

The judgment classes produced real policy. The one I would defend on a whiteboard is the hedge ruling. "The broker said it was covered, supposedly" and "the detention was approved, or something" have nearly identical shape and opposite correct answers. The difference is ownership. A claim attributed to someone else is one the speaker does not own; the hedge is genuine doubt, and jumping in to confirm is helpful, so the agent speaks. A first-person claim with a trailing softener is settled; the hedge is politeness, and the statement rules apply. Attribution markers against first-person assertion are surface features a text model can genuinely learn, which is why this ruling is trainable and not just philosophy.

Then volume. A seeded, pure-code generator expands the policy into training data, with three robustness moves. Complete utterances are re-emitted truncated mid-sentence and labeled wait, because that is exactly what ASR partials look like. Every sample also ships in an ASR-style variant, lowercased with terminal punctuation stripped, so the model cannot cheat off periods. And every contexted sample is also emitted bare, so the utterance carries its own signal. No LLM sits in the generation loop, so the dataset regenerates byte-identically and every line can be audited. The scale-up generator reuses the same template banks verbatim, because the templates are the policy encoding and must not fork, and gets its volume from larger slot pools instead, generated spoken numbers, more cities, more times.

The multilingual bonus is Spanish, chosen for the domain; a large share of US carriers run Spanish-first, and the banks use the register those calls actually have, Mexican-Spanish trucking speech with real Spanglish, "el rate con", "la troca", "el dispatch". The hedge ownership ruling transfers cleanly because Spanish has the same attribution markers, "me dijeron que", "según ellos". The ASR variant also strips inverted punctuation, which matters more than in English because it removes the question cue from the front of the utterance.

Why created instead of found, in one breath. Public conversational corpora carry the wrong distribution (chitchat or meetings, not dispatch calls), no per-class policy labels, and licensing friction, and the thing that actually needed human judgment, the sixty gold cards and the class policies, is small enough to do properly by hand. Provenance for all four data files lives in data/README.md.

## Models, three lanes

The submission trains three models through one recipe and lets the measured curve pick what ships.

The first lane fine-tunes DistilBERT, 66M parameters, English, as a binary classifier over the last agent line plus the caller's words so far. It is the safe recipe the open-source state of the art validates.

The second lane is the one I learned the most from. It trains from random initialization, its own tokenizer trained on the task corpus itself, bilingual, accents kept, feeding a small BERT-shaped encoder of 3.7M parameters. The classification recipe deliberately tracks the fine-tune lane, same loss, same schedule, same selection, same export, so the comparison isolates pretraining. The hypothesis was reasonable: turn-finality lives mostly in surface syntax, connectors, disfluencies, attribution markers, a narrow enough pattern family that a small model might not need pretraining at all.

It failed twice, and each failure bought a finding worth more than a green number. The first attempt used a WordPiece tokenizer, which learned only 1532 pieces because a template corpus has few unique words; text the templates never produced tokenized into walls of unknown tokens, and the model emitted a constant on it, 0.9998 on synthetic validation, a coin-flip 0.50 on the human gold set. The second attempt fixed both mechanical causes, byte-level BPE so nothing can be out of vocabulary, and a template-grouped validation split after catching that a row-level split puts different slot fills of the same template on both sides and measures memorization as generalization. With the mechanics honest, the result got cleaner and the verdict got harder: 0.99 on a held-out slice from the same synthetic distribution, still 0.50 on the gold set. Coverage was no longer the problem; knowledge was.

Then two ingredients moved it, and the amount each moved it is the finding. Mixing in a few hundred real caller turns took gold from 0.50 to 0.994, above every fine-tuned lane, and the mechanism is visible in what did not move: unseen real calls stayed near 0.60, so the real rows had broken the model's collapse on unfamiliar text rather than taught it language. The language came from the second ingredient, a fifteen-minute masked-language-model pretrain of our own, on Wikipedia slices in both languages plus the task corpus, with the tokenizer trained on that combined text. Warm-started from that base, the model reads 0.973 on gold, a perfect Spanish slice, and 0.825 on unseen real calls, with the widest guardrail-safe operating window of any model in the build, sixty admissible thresholds against the incumbent's fifty-eight and the multilingual lane's four. The full curve on unseen real calls, each step one named ingredient: 0.50 from random init, 0.60 adding real data, 0.825 adding our own pretrain, 0.913 where the internet-pretrained fine-tune sits. That last gap is what a few billion tokens of pretraining buy over our twenty-five megabytes, measured. One honest tradeoff inside the lane: pretraining cost a sliver of gold, 0.994 down to 0.973 and one interruption card, to buy the 23 points on real calls, and both versions stay on the scoreboard because that tradeoff is the story.

The third lane fine-tunes multilingual DistilBERT on the bilingual corpus, and the numbers justify it cleanly. On the English gold set it matches the English-only model, 0.960 against 0.958 PR-AUC, so the multilingual vocabulary costs nothing where it matters. On the held-out Spanish slice the English-only model manages 0.911 with recall barely above half at its operating point, while the multilingual fine-tune separates it perfectly. Spanish support is a model swap, not a rebuild, and every other part of the system, policy, generators, evals, serving, carries over unchanged.

The shipped default is picked by a written rule, not taste. Scratch ships only if it lands within about 0.02 PR-AUC of the fine-tune on the gold hard set, keeps false-speak at zero, holds recall within a few points, and does not collapse on the out-of-distribution slice described below. The rule decided against it, narrowly and for one reason. The pretrained scratch model leads or ties on gold, Spanish, guardrail headroom, calibration, and latency, but its 0.825 on unseen real calls is not parity with the fine-tune's 0.913, and the real-call referee is the one production trusts. So the fine-tuned English model ships as the default, 0.949 on gold with zero false-speak and 0.913 on real calls, and the scratch model stands as the fastest, cleanest lane whose remaining gap is the exact thing production data volume closes. The full scoreboard sits at the end of the evaluation section.

## Evaluation

The referee structure matters more than any single number. The frozen gold set grades quality and is never trained on. A separate dev set picks thresholds, so the test referee stays untouched by selection; it is labeled by a certified judge panel, three independent models blind-labeling a shuffled batch, each certified by its agreement with my sixty frozen gold labels before its dev votes count, majority rules, disagreements marked unsure, full provenance recorded, and one caveat stated honestly, the policy doc quotes a handful of gold rulings, so the certification exam was partially open-book and is read accordingly. A regression slice holds every failure found by live probing, as a hard gate that a fix stays fixed. A held-out Spanish slice at a different generator seed grades the bilingual claim.

And then the referee that changed the build. Because everything above lives near one distribution, I pulled sixty real production calls from my own voice agent (the discriminator is exact, calls initiated through Twilio and longer than the watchdog pings) and let them label themselves: the point where the caller actually stopped is a true complete, a mid-turn prefix of the same utterance is a true wait. Four hundred rows, split by call, two thirds available for training augmentation and a third locked as the held-out real-call referee. The labels carry stated noise, the vendor's own turn-taker drew the boundaries, and a random cut can land where a phrase happens to sound complete, so the numbers read as pessimistic bounds, and excluding sentence-boundary cuts moved the headline by only two points, which is how I know the noise is not the story. The story is that the fine-tuned model, 0.96 and zero false-speak on gold, scores 0.61 on real calls with false-speak around forty percent at its operating point. Real callers hesitate, restart, and trail off in ways neither my templates nor my hand-written gold cards do. Every offline referee agreed with each other and the real world disagreed with all of them, which is the single best argument in this document for the monitoring design below, and the fix ran inside the take-home itself: the training-side two thirds went back into the data mix as real-register augmentation, graded on the locked third. It worked on every lane it touched. The shipping fine-tune went from 0.65 to 0.913 on the real referee with recall 0.959. The multilingual lane landed at 0.875. The scratch lane's movement is the pretraining curve told above. One correction to the referee itself is worth naming: turns where the vendor's own turn-taker answered against our written policy would import its behavior into the labels, so the slice builder relabels those to wait, marked and counted; on this pull the count was zero verbatim matches, and the one real poisoning incident traced to softer register patterns instead, caught by a tier-1 gate going red and fixed with data, not string filters.

The raw call content never enters the repo; the slice files stay local and only aggregates appear here.

The verdict follows the written rule: the fine-tuned English model ships as the default because it leads the referee production trusts. The multilingual model is the Spanish-capable variant, guardrail-satisfiable after its data-vintage repair but visibly miscalibrated, so its high gold false-speak at the picked point is stated rather than hidden. The pretrained scratch model is the guardrail-cleanest and fastest lane and the whole measured argument about what pretraining is worth.

Two baselines set the floor. Always-speak-on-pause, which is what a plain VAD timeout does, interrupts on every true wait in the gold set. A punctuation heuristic is respectable on clean text and collapses on ASR-style text. The gates and bands the loop iterates against, including zero tolerance for false-speak on data readouts, live in EVALS.md, with industry anchors verified against LiveKit's and Pipecat's published numbers.

## Serving and latency

FastAPI over ONNX Runtime, dynamically quantized to int8, CPU only, one worker by default. The endpoint returns the probability, the decision at the operating threshold, the threshold in force, and the model latency for every request, and the same process serves a small live probe page that re-scores as you type, which is how several training-data gaps were found. The operating threshold is read from the model directory, so a retrain updates the dial without touching serving code. A Dockerfile builds the serving image with only the int8 model inside.

Measured on my machine, an M5 Pro laptop, single uvicorn worker, int8 on CPU, 1200 requests per level, machine in its late-night post-training thermal state so these read conservative:

| Served artifact | c1 wall p50 | c8 wall p50 | c8 wall p95 | c8 req/s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fine-tuned DistilBERT, 66M | 34.7 ms | 45.5 ms | 57.9 ms | 170 |
| Multilingual DistilBERT, 134M | 34.0 ms | 49.7 ms | 97.2 ms | 147 |
| From-scratch, 7.4M | 7.4 ms | 12.1 ms | 20.7 ms | 588 |

The stress harness is an async client hammering the real endpoint with real payloads at stepped concurrency; at concurrency 32 a single worker saturates, and scaling past it is a worker-count knob, not a model problem. The brief asked for under 100 milliseconds per request. The shipping model clears it at 57.9 ms p95 under eight-way concurrency; the from-scratch lane clears it five times over, and that ratio, seven times faster at a fifth of the hardware per call, is the standing argument for finishing the from-scratch road as production volume closes its remaining accuracy gap.

## Monitoring at a million calls a month

Monitoring here means tracing decisions and observing failures so they convert into evals and guardrails, not dashboards to stare at. Every decision emits a trace, the turn id, the conversation context, the prefix probability trajectory rather than just the final score, the decision, the threshold in force, model latency, and what the caller did next.

That last field is the special thing about this problem. Turn detection grades itself in production, seconds later, for free. A speak decision followed by the caller talking over the agent within about half a second was a false speak. A wait decision followed by dead air until a timeout forced the response was a false wait. Those two rates are the online eval with no annotation cost, and they map one to one onto the offline tradeoff curve. A small weekly human-audited sample, the same labeling booth pointed at sampled production turns, biased toward low-confidence decisions and every barge-in, keeps the auto-labels honest and feeds retraining. Slice everything by language, accent, connection quality, and customer, because turn-taking drifts differently per slice, and alarm on score-distribution drift.

Shipping stays boring. A candidate model first replays the recorded archive offline and shows its disagreements with the incumbent, then shadows live traffic, scoring silently next to the incumbent while disagreement cases get mined for labels. Promotion happens on the measured curve, rollback is a pointer flip to the previous checkpoint.

And the question the brief asks directly, whether we are limited by the datasets previously built. Yes, deliberately, and only until launch. My synthesis encodes policy; production volume supplies distribution. At almost a million calls a month, the handoff from one to the other is the whole monitoring design, and the limit dissolves the day traces start converting into cases.

## The discussion questions, answered in advance

On the limits of the current solution. The modeling ceiling of a text-only detector is prosody. There are utterances whose words are identical whether the caller is finished or inhaling for more, and that signal lives in pitch decline, final lengthening, and breath, none of which reach a text model. The system also inherits the transcriber's cadence, since a decision can only update when a partial arrives. On data, synthetic generation covers the policy but compresses the linguistic diversity of real callers, the gold referee is sixty cards, so per-class readings carry wide intervals, and the policy encodes one operator's judgment, mine, which is a feature for coherence and a limit for customers whose callers behave differently.

On audio against text against multimodal, and why any of them beat VAD. A VAD knows only that sound stopped, so it is forced to buy safety with silence, and every setting of that timeout is the wrong one for half the cases. Text sees what was said, syntax, connectors, attribution, which is most of the turn signal and is cheap to train, fast to serve, and easy to debug, which is why I started there. Audio sees how it was said, catches the prosody cases text cannot, and can act before transcription lands. Multimodal is the real frontier and the direction I would take next, a small audio encoder fused with the text signal at the decision layer, because the eval harness built here, the frozen referee, the cost ratio, the production proxies, transfers to it unchanged. That is the point of building the referee first.

On whether a transcriber could do this job. Yes, and I would build it one of two ways. With Whisper, add an end-of-turn token to the decoder vocabulary and fine-tune on turn-annotated audio, then at inference read the probability of that token against continuation tokens from the same forward pass that produces the transcript, so endpointing becomes a free byproduct of transcription and prosody arrives through the audio encoder. With a streaming RNN-T family model like Parakeet, either train an end-of-utterance token into the transducer vocabulary or hang a small classifier head off the encoder states. The training data both recipes need, audio with true turn boundaries, is exactly what the production self-labeling loop above produces. The tradeoff is coupling, one model now owns two jobs with one latency budget, so I would keep the detector separate while iterating fast and consider distilling it into the transcriber once the policy stabilizes.

On integration into a voice agent. The detector sits between streaming ASR and the LLM trigger. While the caller speaks, partials accumulate; when VAD flags a pause candidate, the detector scores the last agent line plus the transcript so far, and the probability maps to an endpoint delay, a high score commits after a short guard, a middling score waits longer, a low score holds toward a hard ceiling and re-scores on every new partial. Barge-in handling is untouched. The model adds single-digit milliseconds co-located next to the orchestrator, well inside any partial cadence. Two refinements pay for themselves quickly, smoothing across the prefix trajectory so the decision does not flap between partials, and speculative generation, starting LLM inference the moment the score goes high and cancelling if the caller resumes, which converts detector confidence directly into answer latency.

## Assumptions, written down

The 1:5 cost ratio is a documented operating choice, not a law; it is one number, and re-deriving the threshold for a different ratio is a one-line change. Inputs arrive ASR-style, lowercased, no terminal punctuation, and the last agent utterance is available as context. The decision is binary, speak or wait; how long a response should be, and whether an acknowledgement ends a call, are downstream policies and deliberately out of scope. Languages covered are English and Spanish. The latency budget is 100 milliseconds end to end on CPU, no GPU assumed anywhere.
